# Debate Assignment
<!-- **Pitching Competing Designs for Recommender Systems** -->

In this assignment, you will analyse and defend two competing statements pertaining to a recommender system (or its implementation) that address the same problem in fundamentally different ways.

Each debate revolves around a single design question about how a recommender system should be implemented. One team will argue in favour of the statement, while the opposing team will argue for an alternative decision. Both positions should be treated as plausible, defensible implementation/programming choices, each with distinct societal, ethical, and technical consequences.

Students work in debate teams (assigned during tutorials). Each team receives:

1.	a scenario describing a recommender system context, and
2.	a specific position (e.g., SHOULD vs. SHOULD NOT) that they must defend.

Your task is not to argue your personal opinion, but rather to justify your assigned design choice using course concepts and literature, articulate the trade-offs of your approach, and critically evaluate the risks and limitations of the alternative design.

The purpose of the debates is to simulate a real-world situation in which different stakeholders (news rooms, companies, regulators, users, ...) advocate for different system designs because of their different interests. There is no objectively “right” position – rather, your job is to convince the jury that the position you are arguing for is the more justifiable one.

Debate Format
*	Opening statement (2 min)
    *	Each team presents its proposed system design and explains why it is the better solution for the given scenario. Do not yet respond to the opposing team.
*	Regroup (3 min)
*	Open debate (5-10 min)
    *  	Incl. questions from the opposing team and the jury (other teams + instructor), focusing on technical, ethical, and societal implications.
*	Regroup (2 min)
*	Closing statement (2 min)
    *	Teams summarize their position, explicitly responding to the strongest arguments raised by the opposing team.

The jury will decide the outcome based on the quality of the arguments, use of course material, and clarity of presentation. Most importantly, the quality of the arguments is not based on justifications outside of course literature and materials, or on personal agreement with a position.

Not every member of a team has to participate actively in the debate itself (although everyone is expected to help with the preparation). It is up to the teams to come up with a useful division of tasks. We encourage you to develop specific tasks for specific people, from researching your side’s arguments to developing expected opponent’s arguments (and rebuttals to these), to wording opening/closing statements, to delivery during the debate, etc. 

Have fun!
